Case study 01 · Subscription platform
MySQL · Power BI · Simulated dataset, 7 tables

Why a subscription platform's revenue slipped

"Revenue fell 5.75% (₦2.18M) in Q4 2025. Was the cause fewer customers or lower spend per customer, and what is driving it?"

Power BI dashboard

Approach

  1. Cleaned seven messy tables in MySQL: standardised text, staged working copies, removed duplicates with ROW_NUMBER(), converted text dates, and handled blanks and negative amounts.
  2. Decomposed revenue quarter by quarter with LAG() into customer count and average spend per customer. The decline came from spend, not headcount.
  3. Drilled into spend by region, plan tier, billing cycle, acquisition channel, device and app version.
  4. Compared churn with acquisition, which exposed a leaky bucket.
  5. Tested six hypotheses (network issues, economic pressure, bugs and lags, plan dissatisfaction, conversion quality, misinformation), then measured the churn uplift of each combination in percentage points.

Findings

  • A retention problem, not an acquisition one. Churn of 83.67% outpaced an acquisition rate of 82.76%.
  • Friction compounds. Churned users rose with every extra problem faced: 5 with none, 113 with one, 213 with two, 454 with three, 598 with four.
  • Weakest segments: iOS users on app version 4.2.1 in Jos, Ilorin and Kano, and the Standard tier. Abuja, Enugu and version 4.1.0 held up. Heavy users (over 2 hours and 4 sessions) were frustrated most by platform lag.
Issue combinationChurn rateVs. others
Network issues + dissatisfied91.77%+7.35 pp
Economic constraint + dissatisfied89.72%+6.30 pp
Network issues + bugs / lags89.57%+5.62 pp
Misinformed + dissatisfied69.23%−14.61 pp

Recommendations

  • Engineering: ship a fix for the performance bottlenecks and error codes seen in pre-churn sessions on iOS 4.2.1, prioritising heavy users.
  • Growth: shift acquisition spend towards more stable, less economically pressured regions such as Abuja and Enugu, to win longer-retaining customers.
  • Product: add in-app tutorials and a clear breakdown of each plan tier to fix how the Standard tier's value is perceived.

Findings are associations from a simulated dataset; acquisition cost data was not available.
Case study 02 · E-commerce retailer
MySQL · Power BI · Simulated dataset, 7 tables

Where an online retailer's customers went

"Revenue slipped from Q1 to Q2 2026, and 302 of 771 Q1 customers never came back. What drove them away, and what should the business fix first?"

Power BI dashboard

Approach

  1. Cleaned seven raw tables through SQL views: removed duplicates, set invalid dates to NULL, validated prices with a regex check, and standardised region names.
  2. Ran data-quality checks (bad dates, unusable prices, orphan orders, returns without records) before any analysis.
  3. Built a fixed churn group: customers who completed an order in Q1 but not in Q2.
  4. Flagged each customer for what they experienced in Q1 (late delivery, return, cancellation, falling stock, a price rise of 10% or more, failed payment) and compared churn between flagged and unflagged groups.
  5. Checked overlap between issues, marked small groups as unreliable, and kept association separate from causation throughout.

Recommendations

  • Tighten delivery: fix the regions where delays cluster, and set promised dates the network can actually meet.
  • Restock first: prioritise the products whose availability fell from Q1 to Q2.
  • Win customers back: follow up every return and cancellation within the quarter with a recovery offer.
  • Leave pricing and payments alone: neither explains the churn, so changing them would spend effort in the wrong place.

Limitations: findings are associations, not proven causes. Availability is measured from monthly closing stock, and a single prior year cannot fully rule out seasonality.
Case study 03 · NGOs & insurers
MySQL · Exploratory analysis · Simulated dataset, 3 sources

Climate shocks & food insecurity

"Under which climate conditions, and in which regions, does food insecurity concentrate, so NGOs know where to act first and insurers know how to set payout triggers?"

Approach

  1. Joined three sources (crop-yield and rainfall anomalies, the El Niño index, and IPC food-insecurity assessments) by quarter and region into a single SQL view: 60 quarters across four regions.
  2. Classified every quarter as El Niño, La Niña or stable, and compared rainfall, yield loss and the population in crisis, region by region.
  3. Ranked regional vulnerability and the severity at each IPC phase.
  4. Built two practical models in SQL: a parametric-insurance payout simulation, and an early-warning risk tier for NGO operations.

Findings

  • El Niño drives crisis in three regions: Central America, Southeast Asia and Southern Africa.
  • East Africa is the exception. El Niño brings it more rain; the drier La Niña periods bring crop loss and crisis.
  • Southern Africa carries the heaviest burden: the most people in crisis on average, the highest yield loss and the highest cumulative funding.
  • Stable climate is no safeguard. Crises still occurred in neutral quarters, including a −28.19% rainfall deficit in Southern Africa.

Recommendations

  • For NGOs: prioritise Southern Africa. Treat El Niño as the alert in three regions and dry La Niña as the alert in East Africa. Keep reserves that don't depend on a climate signal, and record when aid arrives relative to IPC phase changes.
  • For insurers: design triggers region by region, using rainfall deviation and historical yield loss. Don't wait for an official El Niño or La Niña declaration; local rainfall thresholds belong in every trigger.

Limitations: these are exploratory associations, not proof that any climate condition caused the crises. Funding is cumulative, with no record of whether aid arrived before or after a crisis.
